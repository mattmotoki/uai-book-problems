# 1. Preliminaries

Fix

and let \(Q=N(M)\) be the probability measure generated by \(q\).

Q(x)\ge \frac{M(x)}{M(\epsilon)}\ge M(x).

When the environment is \(P\), write

A_t(P,Q)=\mathbb E_P\sum_b|q(b\mid X_{<t})-p(b\mid X_{<t})|,
\]
and define \(S_t(P,Q)\) analogously using squared differences. For the fixed environment \(\mu\), abbreviate these to \(A_t,S_t\).

For binary probability vectors,
\[
s_t=\frac12a_t^2,\qquad
S_t\ge\frac12A_t^2,\qquad S_t\le A_t.
\tag{2}
\]

I use the coding facts from §1 of the attached report:
\[
\sum_nw_n\le1,\qquad w_n\ge c_0(n+1)^{-2},
\tag{3}
\]
and
\[
g\text{ lower semicomputable},\quad g\ge0,\quad \sum_ng(n)\le1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
g(n)\le C_gw_n.
\tag{4}
\]

# 2. The central finite lemma

This lemma allows the forced discrepancies to be placed at **selected coordinates with arbitrary gaps between them**. It is the main improvement over the earlier block argument.

## Lemma 1 — selective forcing

Let \(P,Q\) be full-support probability laws on binary strings of length \(N\), with conditional kernels \(p,q\). Let
\[
F=\{d_1<\cdots<d_m\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}.
\]
At each selected time, prescribe a history-dependent bit \(f_t(x)\) satisfying
\[
p(f_t(x)\mid x)\le\rho<1.
\tag{5}
\]

Define \(\nu\) to follow \(P\) at unselected coordinates and output \(f_t(x)\) deterministically at selected coordinates.

If
\[
Q(z)\ge r\nu(z)\qquad(|z|=N)
\tag{6}
\]
for some \(0<r\le1\), then
\[
\boxed{\sum_{t\in F}A_t(P,Q)\ge2(r-\rho^m).}
\tag{7}
\]

### Proof

Define \(\widehat Q\) to use \(q\) at selected coordinates and \(p\) everywhere else. Let \(E\) be the event that all prescribed choices are satisfied.

Successive conditioning gives
\[
P(E)\le\rho^m.
\tag{8}
\]

The key assertion is
\[
\widehat Q(E)\ge r.
\tag{9}
\]

Here is a proof using only finite tree induction and rational inequalities.

For a prefix \(x\) with \(\nu(x)>0\), let \(h(x)\) be the \(\widehat Q\)-conditional probability that all remaining prescribed choices will be satisfied. Prove backwards that
\[
h(x)\ge r\frac{\nu(x)}{Q(x)}.
\tag{10}
\]

At a terminal feasible string, \(h(x)=1\), so (10) is exactly the domination assumption.

At a selected node, writing \(f=f_{|x|+1}(x)\),
\[
\nu(xf)=\nu(x),\qquad Q(xf)=Q(x)q(f\mid x).
\]
Therefore
\[
h(x)=q(f\mid x)h(xf)
\ge r\frac{\nu(x)}{Q(x)}.
\]

At an unselected node,
\[
\nu(xb)=\nu(x)p(b\mid x),\qquad
Q(xb)=Q(x)q(b\mid x),
\]
and hence
\[
h(x)
=\sum_bp(b\mid x)h(xb)
\ge r\frac{\nu(x)}{Q(x)}
\sum_b\frac{p(b\mid x)^2}{q(b\mid x)}.
\]
For binary probability vectors,
\[
\frac{p_0^2}{q_0}+\frac{p_1^2}{q_1}-1
=\frac{(p_0-q_0)^2}{q_0q_1}\ge0.
\tag{11}
\]
This closes the induction. At the root, both prefix masses equal one, proving (9).

Thus
\[
\|P-\widehat Q\|_1
\ge2\bigl(\widehat Q(E)-P(E)\bigr)
\ge2(r-\rho^m).
\tag{12}
\]

Finally, telescope the hybrid laws that use \(P\) through coordinate \(k\) and \(\widehat Q\) afterwards. Their adjacent distances are the \(P\)-expected one-step conditional distances. Unselected coordinates contribute zero, so
\[
\|P-\widehat Q\|_1
\le\sum_{t\in F}A_t(P,Q).
\tag{13}
\]
Combining (12) and (13) proves the lemma. \(\square\)

**Confidence: high.** This is an especially suitable Lean target. Its central numerical ingredient is exactly (11), and its central structural ingredient is backward induction on a finite binary tree.

## Lemma 2 — changing the environment slightly

For a fixed predictor \(q\) and any two environment laws \(P,R\),
\[
\boxed{
|A_t(P,Q)-A_t(R,Q)|
\le
\|P_{t-1}-R_{t-1}\|_1+\|P_t-R_t\|_1.
}
\tag{14}
\]
In particular, if \(R=(1-\delta)P+\delta L\), where \(L\) is a probability measure, then
\[
|A_t(P,Q)-A_t(R,Q)|\le4\delta.
\tag{15}
\]

**Proof.** Write the expected error without conditional probabilities of the environment:
\[
A_t(P,Q)=
\sum_{|x|=t-1}\sum_b
|P(x)q(b\mid x)-P(xb)|.
\tag{16}
\]
This formula remains valid at \(P\)-null histories.

Apply the reverse triangle inequality and sum. The terms involving prefix masses sum to \(\|P_{t-1}-R_{t-1}\|_1\), since \(\sum_bq(b\mid x)=1\); the remaining terms give the length-\(t\) marginal distance. Each distance for the stated mixture is at most \(2\delta\). \(\square\)

**Confidence: high.** This is a finite-sum lemma. It removes the computability difficulty at null histories.

# 3. Lower bounds along every effective selection

## Theorem 1 — logarithmic blocks of selected coordinates

Let \(M\in\mathcal A\), let \(\mu\) be any computable probability measure, and let
\[
d_1<d_2<\cdots
\]
be any total computable increasing sequence.

Set
\[
\ell_n=6\lceil\log_2(n+2)\rceil,\qquad
B_0=0,\qquad B_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\ell_k,
\]
and
\[
F_n=\{d_j:B_{n-1}<j\le B_n\}.
\tag{17}
\]

Then there exist \(c>0,n_0\) such that
\[
\forall n\ge n_0\ \exists t\in F_n:
\qquad
\boxed{A_t\ge cw_t,\qquad S_t\ge\frac{c^2}{2}w_t^2.}
\tag{18}
\]
The same time satisfies both inequalities.

### Proof

First smooth the environment:
\[
\mu_n=(1-2^{-n})\mu+2^{-n}\lambda.
\tag{19}
\]
These measures have full support and uniformly computable conditionals. A cylinder of length \(k\) has the known lower bound \(2^{-n-k}\), so division to compute a conditional is effective.

At each \(t\in F_n\), computably choose a bit with \(\mu_n\)-conditional probability strictly below \(2/3\). At least one bit has probability at most \(1/2\), so approximation eventually certifies a suitable strict inequality.

Let \(\nu_n\) force those choices and follow \(\mu_n\) at every unselected coordinate. This is a uniformly computable family.

The mixture
\[
R=\sum_{n\ge1}w_n\nu_n
\]
is a lower semicomputable semimeasure. Universality and (1) supply **one constant** \(a\in(0,1]\) such that
\[
Q\ge M\ge aR\ge aw_n\nu_n
\qquad\text{for every }n.
\tag{20}
\]

Apply Lemma 1 at horizon \(\max F_n\):
\[
\sum_{t\in F_n}A_t(\mu_n,Q)
\ge2aw_n-2(2/3)^{\ell_n}.
\tag{21}
\]
Lemma 2 gives
\[
\sum_{t\in F_n}A_t(\mu,Q)
\ge
2aw_n-2(2/3)^{\ell_n}-4\ell_n2^{-n}.
\tag{22}
\]

Now
\[
(2/3)^{\ell_n}
\le(n+2)^{-6\log_2(3/2)},
\]
where \(6\log_2(3/2)>3>2\). Using (3),
\[
(2/3)^{\ell_n}=o(w_n),
\qquad
\ell_n2^{-n}=o(w_n).
\]
Therefore, eventually,
\[
\sum_{t\in F_n}A_t\ge aw_n.
\tag{23}
\]

The sets \(F_n\) are uniformly computable, finite, and pairwise disjoint. Hence
\[
g(n)=\sum_{t\in F_n}w_t
\]
is lower semicomputable and satisfies \(\sum_ng(n)\le1\). By (4),
\[
\sum_{t\in F_n}w_t\le Cw_n
\tag{24}
\]
for some fixed \(C>0\).

Equations (23)–(24) imply that some \(t\in F_n\) has \(A_t/w_t\ge a/C\). Set \(c=a/C\) and apply \(S_t\ge A_t^2/2\). \(\square\)

**Confidence: high after independent proof checks.** The main checks for formalization are the single domination constant in (20), disjointness in (24), and the null-history-safe identity (16). No convergence modulus for \(M\) is assumed.

## Corollary 1 — every infinite c.e. set contains witnesses

For every \(M\in\mathcal A\), every computable \(\mu\), and every infinite computably enumerable \(D\subseteq\mathbb N_{\ge1}\), some \(c>0\) gives
\[
A_t\ge cw_t,\qquad S_t\ge\frac{c^2}{2}w_t^2
\]
simultaneously at infinitely many \(t\in D\).

**Proof.** From an enumeration of \(D\), select a computable increasing subsequence: after selecting \(d_j\), wait for an enumerated member exceeding it. Infinitude guarantees termination. Apply Theorem 1. \(\square\)

Thus
\[
\limsup_{\substack{t\to\infty\\t\in D}}\frac{A_t}{w_t}>0,
\qquad
\limsup_{\substack{t\to\infty\\t\in D}}\frac{S_t}{w_t^2}>0.
\tag{25}
\]
The constants may depend on \(D\).

## Corollary 2 — logarithmic gaps in ordinary time

For every fixed \(M,\mu\), there are \(c>0,C>0,T\) such that
\[
\forall u\ge T\ \exists t\in
\{u,\ldots,u+\lceil C\log_2(u+2)\rceil\}:
\quad
A_t\ge cw_t,\quad S_t\ge\frac{c^2}{2}w_t^2.
\tag{26}
\]

Moreover, there are
\[
\Omega(N/\log N)
\tag{27}
\]
such witnesses up to \(N\).

**Proof.** Take \(d_j=j\). If \(u\) lies in the \(n\)-th partition block, the next complete block lies within \(\ell_n+\ell_{n+1}=O(\log u)\) steps and contains a witness. Also,
\[
B_n\le6n\lceil\log_2(n+2)\rceil=O(n\log n).
\]
Every late block contributes a distinct witness. \(\square\)

These gaps may still grow. The conclusion is a logarithmic upper bound on the gaps, not bounded gaps or positive density.

# 4. Stronger squared errors for uniformly stochastic measures

The selective-forcing construction also permits the stronger squared-error scale from the earlier report.

## Lemma 3 — finite event discrepancy forces squared error

Let \(P,\widehat Q\) be probability laws through time \(N\), with
\[
p(b\mid x)\ge\varepsilon>0.
\]
Suppose their conditional kernels agree outside \(F\). If an event \(E\) satisfies
\[
\widehat Q(E)\ge r,\qquad P(E)\le\delta,
\]
then
\[
\boxed{
\sum_{t\in F}S_t(P,\widehat Q)
\ge\frac{\varepsilon^2}{8}(r-2\delta).
}
\tag{28}
\]

### Proof

Under \(P\),
\[
L_k=\frac{\widehat Q(X_{1:k})}{P(X_{1:k})}
\]
is a martingale with \(L_0=1\). Let \(\tau\) be its first crossing of \(2\), and let \(C=\{\tau\le N\}\).

On \(C^c\), \(L_N<2\), so
\[
\widehat Q(E\cap C^c)\le2P(E)\le2\delta.
\]
Therefore
\[
\widehat Q(C)\ge r-2\delta.
\tag{29}
\]

At a first crossing,
\[
L_\tau\le2/\varepsilon,
\]
because the previous likelihood ratio is below \(2\), and a one-step multiplier is at most \(1/\varepsilon\). Summing over first-crossing cylinders,
\[
\widehat Q(C)
=\mathbb E_P[L_\tau\mathbf1_C]
\le(2/\varepsilon)P(C).
\]
Thus
\[
P(C)\ge(\varepsilon/2)(r-2\delta).
\tag{30}
\]

The stopped variance is at least \(P(C)\), since \((L_\tau-1)^2\ge1\) on \(C\). Orthogonality of finite martingale increments gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_P(L_{\tau\wedge N}-1)^2
&=\sum_{t=1}^N
\mathbb E_P\!\left[
\mathbf1_{\{\tau\ge t\}}L_{t-1}^2
\sum_b\frac{(\widehat q(b\mid X_{<t})-p(b\mid X_{<t}))^2}
{p(b\mid X_{<t})}
\right]\\
&\le\frac4\varepsilon\sum_{t\in F}S_t(P,\widehat Q).
\end{aligned}
\]
Outside \(F\), the increments vanish. Combine this with (30). \(\square\)

**Confidence: high.** This is a finite stopping and variance calculation. The overshoot bound and the vanishing of unselected increments are the important Lean checks.

## Theorem 2 — \(S_t\gtrsim w_t\) along every effective selection

Suppose \(\mu\) is computable and
\[
\varepsilon\le\mu(b\mid x)\le1-\varepsilon
\tag{31}
\]
at every positive history.

For every \(M\in\mathcal A\) and every computable increasing selection \(d_j\), using the batches \(F_n\) from Theorem 1, there exist \(c>0,n_0\) such that
\[
\forall n\ge n_0\ \exists t\in F_n:
\qquad\boxed{S_t\ge cw_t.}
\tag{32}
\]
The same time has \(A_t\ge cw_t\).

### Proof

Uniform nondegeneracy implies full support, so construct the alternatives directly from \(\mu\), without smoothing. Force a bit of conditional probability below \(2/3\) at each selected time and otherwise follow \(\mu\).

As before,
\[
Q\ge aw_n\nu_n.
\]
The backward induction in Lemma 1 gives a projected law \(\widehat Q_n\) and an event \(E_n\) satisfying
\[
\widehat Q_n(E_n)\ge aw_n,\qquad
\mu(E_n)\le(2/3)^{\ell_n}.
\]
Moreover, \(\widehat Q_n\) uses \(q\) on \(F_n\) and \(\mu\)'s kernel elsewhere.

Lemma 3 gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{t\in F_n}S_t(\mu,Q)
&\ge\frac{\varepsilon^2}{8}
\left(aw_n-2(2/3)^{\ell_n}\right)\\
&\ge\frac{a\varepsilon^2}{16}w_n
\end{aligned}
\]
eventually. Apply (24) and weighted averaging. Finally, \(A_t\ge S_t\). \(\square\)

Consequently, for every infinite c.e. \(D\),
\[
\boxed{
\limsup_{\substack{t\to\infty\\t\in D}}\frac{S_t}{w_t}>0.
}
\tag{33}
\]
The logarithmic-window and witness-count conclusions hold at this stronger scale.

**Confidence: high.** The fair coin is the special case \(\varepsilon=1/2\). This remains a recurrence result, not an eventual bound at every time.

# 5. Finite error concentration is possible even in \(\mathcal B\)

All errors in this section are measured against the fair coin.

## Theorem 3 — exact symmetry at any prescribed finite set

For every finite \(F\subseteq\mathbb N_{\ge1}\) and every \(V\in\mathcal A\), there is \(V_F\in\mathcal A\) such that
\[
A_t=S_t=0\qquad(t\in F).
\tag{34}
\]
If \(V\in\mathcal B\), the constructed \(V_F\) belongs to \(\mathcal B\).

### Proof

For \(G\subseteq F\), let \(\tau_G\) flip the coordinates in \(G\), and define
\[
V_F(x)=2^{-|F|}\sum_{G\subseteq F}V(\tau_Gx).
\tag{35}
\]
Each summand is a lower semicomputable semimeasure. The empty-mask summand gives
\[
V_F\ge2^{-|F|}V,
\]
so universal domination remains.

For \(t\in F\), toggling \(t\)'s membership in \(G\) pairs the summands for \(x0\) and \(x1\), where \(|x|=t-1\). Thus
\[
V_F(x0)=V_F(x1),
\]
and the normalized prediction is exactly \(1/2\).

For \(\mathcal B\), a machine first reads a fixed-length random header selecting \(G\), then simulates the machine for \(V\) and flips the selected output coordinates. The zero-mask branch preserves universality by adjunction. \(\square\)

## Theorem 4 — one time can carry all the error in a finite block

For every nonempty finite \(I\) and every \(j\in I\), there is \(M\in\mathcal B\) such that
\[
A_t=S_t=0\quad(t\in I\setminus\{j\}),
\tag{36}
\]
while
\[
\boxed{A_j\ge5/7,\qquad S_j\ge25/98.}
\tag{37}
\]

### Proof

Set \(F=I\setminus\{j\}\), choose \(V\in\mathcal B\), and construct \(V_F\). Let \(\nu_j\) have independent fair bits except for a deterministic zero at time \(j\). Put
\[
M=\frac78\nu_j+\frac18V_F.
\]
A three-bit dispatcher realizes this mixture and retains a universal branch.

Both components have equal sibling masses at every \(t\in F\), proving (36).

For \(|x|=j-1\),
\[
\nu_j(x0)=\lambda(x),\qquad \nu_j(x1)=0.
\]
Therefore
\[
q_M(1\mid x)
=\frac{V_F(x1)}
{7\lambda(x)+V_F(x0)+V_F(x1)}
\le\frac{V_F(x1)}{7\lambda(x)}.
\]
Averaging,
\[
\mathbb E_\lambda q_M(1\mid X_{<j})
\le\frac17\sum_{|x|=j-1}V_F(x1)\le\frac17.
\]
Since \(a_j(x)=|1-2q_M(1\mid x)|\),
\[
A_j\ge1-2\mathbb E_\lambda q_M(1\mid X_{<j})
\ge\frac57.
\]
Then \(S_j\ge A_j^2/2\ge25/98\). \(\square\)

**Confidence: high for both finite constructions.**

The quantifier distinction is decisive:
\[
\forall I\ \forall j\in I\ \exists M.
\]
This does not construct one fixed \(M\) with infinitely many such blocks. The universality constant can deteriorate with \(|I|\), and (E) permits both its constant and threshold to depend on \(M\).

Thus this defeats an unconditional finite spreading lemma. It does not defeat every possible global proof using a fixed prior and its complexity constants.

# 6. Where the proposed routes break

## 6.1 Averaging and monotonicity

Theorem 4 shows that a finite block’s discrepancy can be concentrated at one time. The logarithmic-window theorem limits the gaps between witnesses globally, but still selects some time rather than controlling every time.

There is also no eventual-monotonicity principle for \(A_t\) or \(S_t\).

Indeed, dominance gives \(\sum_tS_t<\infty\), and then (2) gives \(\sum_tA_t^2<\infty\). If \(S_t\) were eventually nonincreasing, summability would imply \(S_t=O(1/t)\). If \(A_t\) were eventually nonincreasing, it would imply \(A_t=O(t^{-1/2})\).

Apply Corollary 1 to \(D=\{2^n:n\ge1\}\). Since
\[
K(2^n)\le K(n)+O(1),
\]
infinitely many such times satisfy
\[
A_{2^n}\ge c_1(n+1)^{-2},\qquad
S_{2^n}\ge c_2(n+1)^{-4}.
\]
These contradict either monotone rate. The cumulative bound used here is the standard dominance/KL convergence bound. [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0709.1516?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

## 6.2 Exact infinite symmetrization

For the fair coin, \(A_t=0\) means exact fairness at every history of length \(t-1\). If this happened infinitely often,
\[
Q(0^N)\le2^{-\#\{t\le N:A_t=0\}}\longrightarrow0.
\]
But universality gives \(M(0^N)\ge c>0\), by domination of the point mass at \(0^\infty\), and \(Q\ge M\).

Thus a counterexample must use **small nonzero errors**, preserving exceptional histories carrying the computable atoms.

As in the earlier report, the positive atom gives \(q(0\mid0^{t-1})\to1\), and hence eventually
\[
A_t\ge2^{-t},\qquad S_t\ge2^{-t}/4.
\]
These remain much weaker than the scales in (E).

## 6.3 The quiet times must evade every effective infinite selection

An infinite set is called *immune* if it contains no infinite c.e. subset. The new theorem implies:

- If \(A_t/w_t\to0\) along an infinite \(D\), then \(D\) is immune.
- If \(S_t/w_t^2\to0\) along \(D\), then \(D\) is immune.
- Under uniform nondegeneracy, even \(S_t/w_t\to0\) along \(D\) forces immunity.

For example, a computably listed sequence \(t_1<t_2<\cdots\) satisfying
\[
A_{t_n}\le2^{-n}w_{t_n}
\]
is impossible for one fixed universal prior.

**This is the precise remaining gap:** failure of an eventual lower bound supplies an increasingly quiet subsequence, but that subsequence need not contain an infinite c.e. subset.

Density one does not fix this. To see this directly, enumerate all c.e. sets \(W_e\). For each \(e\), if an element \(x\ge2^e\) appears in \(W_e\), put one such element into \(H\), once only for that \(e\). Then \(H\) meets every infinite c.e. set, while
\[
|H\cap\{1,\ldots,N\}|\le1+\lfloor\log_2N\rfloor.
\]
Its complement has density one and is immune.

One can also adjoin to \(H\) a computable density-zero set with logarithmic gaps. The complement is still density one and immune. Thus the immunity and logarithmic-gap restrictions are logically compatible with a density-one quiet set. This is only a set-theoretic observation, not a semimeasure construction.

## 6.4 A finite truncation operation and the missing infinite construction

There is a concrete effective partial symmetrization worth retaining.

At a target depth \(t\), set
\[
a=M(x0),\quad b=M(x1),\quad h=H\lambda(x)/2,
\]
and replace the child masses by
\[
a'=\max(a,\min(b,h)),\qquad
b'=\max(b,\min(a,h)).
\tag{38}
\]
These remain lower semicomputable. When \(a,b\le h\), they become equal.

The added mass is exactly
\[
a'+b'-a-b
=\min\bigl(|a-b|,(h-\min(a,b))_+\bigr).
\tag{39}
\]
Update ancestors upwards by taking the maximum of their old value and the new child sum. The root increase is at most the sum of (39), which is at most
\[
\sum_{|x|=t-1}|M(x0)-M(x1)|\le1.
\]
Dividing the resulting semimeasure by \(2\) restores the root bound and preserves its normalized predictions and universal domination.

Nonzero fair-coin error remains only where
\[
\max(a,b)>H\lambda(x)/2.
\]
The fair-coin probability of these histories is at most \(2/H\), so at the target time
\[
A_t\le2/H,\qquad S_t\le1/H.
\tag{40}
\]

For **fixed** \(M,H\), the total correction cost tends to zero as the target depth increases. Let
\[
D_k=M(X_{1:k})/\lambda(X_{1:k}),
\]
a convergent nonnegative supermartingale. The expected cost is bounded by
\[
\mathbb E_\lambda\min(|D_t-D_{t-1}|,H)\longrightarrow0,
\]
by bounded convergence.

The unresolved steps are substantial:

1. Fixed-cap convergence does not control caps increasing with the target time. For instance, \(H_t\asymp t^5\) would make (40) beat both required coding scales, but no summable bound on those correction costs has been established.
2. Later corrections can destroy earlier equalities.
3. An effective list of increasingly successful final times would contradict Corollary 1.
4. The max/min construction directly gives a member of \(\mathcal A\); membership in \(\mathcal B\) would require a separate effective machine construction.

I do not have the root-mass accounting and preservation argument needed to complete this route.

The earlier report’s eventual special cases remain valid. If \(\rho_t\) is the \(\mu\)-mass of histories with a deterministic next-bit conditional, the all-history symbol floor gives
\[
A_t\ge2c_M\rho_tw_t,\qquad
S_t\ge2c_M^2\rho_tw_t^2.
\]
At each such history, the wrong bit has true probability zero and predicted probability at least \(c_Mw_t\); integration proves the inequalities. Thus \(\liminf_t\rho_t>0\) establishes the eventual conclusions for that environment. Hutter’s all-history symbol floor supports this argument, but does not itself bound deviation from a stochastic conditional. [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0709.1516?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

A counterexample in \(\mathcal A\) also cannot automatically be moved into \(\mathcal B\) by adding a fixed universal branch. The resulting normalized conditional is a history-dependent convex combination of the two predictors. A fixed admixture need not preserve errors of order \(o(w_t)\) or \(o(w_t^2)\). Under the conventional machine definition, the empty-string value must also be set to one; that adjustment leaves normalized predictions unchanged.
