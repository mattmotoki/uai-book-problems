## 1. Setup and the constraint on any counterexample

Write a history as
\[
h=(a_1,r_1)\cdots(a_t,r_t),
\]
where \(a_i\in\{0,1\}\) is the prediction and \(r_i\) indicates whether it was correct. Decode its truth string by
\[
d(h)_i=
\begin{cases}
a_i,&r_i=1,\\
1-a_i,&r_i=0.
\end{cases}
\]

Let \(A=\xi^{AI}\) be a universal lower semicomputable chronological semimeasure. Thus, for each action \(a\),
\[
A(h(a,0))+A(h(a,1))\le A(h).
\]
The horizon-one greedy policy selects
\[
\pi_A(h)\in\arg\max_a A(h(a,1)),
\]
with any fixed deterministic maximizing tie rule.

Let \(M=\xi^{SP}\) be a universal binary sequence semimeasure, let \(h_x\) be the actual greedy history generated by truth prefix \(x\), and put
\[
E_A(x,y)=\text{number of errors on \(y\) after \(x\)},\qquad
R_M(x,y)=\frac{M(x)}{M(xy)}.
\]
All finite \(M\)-values are positive.

Throughout, \(K\) means ordinary halting prefix complexity, with a finite, end-marked condition. The coding-theorem applications below are to **discrete** semimeasures, including conditional discrete semimeasures. This is the standard effective prefix-coding framework. ### Proposition 1: finite error budget and necessary mass separation

For every fixed universal \(A,M\), there is \(c>0\) such that, for every \(x\) and nonempty \(y\),
\[
\boxed{(E_A(x,y)+1)cM(xy)\le A(h_x).}
\tag{1}
\]
Consequently,
\[
\boxed{
\frac{E_A(x,y)+1}{R_M(x,y)}
\le \frac{A(h_x)}{cM(x)}
\le \frac1{cM(x)}.
}
\tag{2}
\]

In particular, any family satisfying
\[
\frac{E_A(x_j,y_j)}{R_M(x_j,y_j)}\longrightarrow\infty
\]
must also satisfy
\[
M(x_j)\longrightarrow0,
\qquad
\frac{A(h_{x_j})}{M(x_j)}\longrightarrow\infty.
\tag{3}
\]

**Proof.** The passive lift
\[
L_M(h)=M(d(h))
\]
is a lower semicomputable chronological semimeasure, so universality supplies
\[
A(h)\ge cM(d(h)).
\]

At an erroneous cycle, with chosen action \(a_i\) and true bit \(z_i=1-a_i\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
A(h_{i-1})-A(h_i)
&\ge A(h_{i-1}(a_i,1))\\
&\ge A(h_{i-1}(z_i,1))\\
&\ge cM(z_{1:i})\\
&\ge cM(xy).
\end{aligned}
\]
The second inequality is greediness. At a correct cycle, mass does not increase. Therefore
\[
E_A(x,y)cM(xy)\le A(h_x)-A(h_{xy}).
\]
The terminal lower bound \(A(h_{xy})\ge cM(xy)\) supplies the extra \(1\), proving (1). Dividing proves (2), and then (3). \(\square\)

**Confidence:** high. **Novelty:** the established finite-budget argument, with its necessary-condition consequence.

This answers part of “where must the errors come from?” Each mistake costs at least a fixed multiple of the terminal SP mass. A counterexample needs a starting history whose AI mass is much larger than its SP mass, and it must spend that excess through many mistakes.

## 2. The passive SP predictor: a complete sharp result

Let
\[
P_M(u)\in\arg\max_b M(ub)
\]
and let \(E_M(x,y)\) count its mistakes.

### Theorem 2: passive halving bound

For every binary semimeasure \(M\), every maximizing tie rule, and all finite \(x,y\) with \(M(xy)>0\),
\[
\boxed{M(xy)\le 2^{-E_M(x,y)}M(x).}
\tag{5}
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{
E_M(x,y)\le\log_2 R_M(x,y),\qquad
E_M(x,y)+1\le R_M(x,y).
}
\tag{6}
\]
One also has
\[
E_M(x,y)\le \tfrac12 R_M(x,y).
\tag{7}
\]

**Proof.** At a mistake, if the true next bit is \(b\), greediness gives
\[
M(ub)\le M(u(1-b)).
\]
Consequently,
\[
2M(ub)\le M(ub)+M(u(1-b))\le M(u).
\]
At a correct step, use \(M(ub)\le M(u)\). Multiplying along the suffix proves (5).

Taking logarithms gives the first inequality in (6). For integers \(e\ge0\), \(2^e\ge e+1\), proving the second. For \(e\ge1\), \(2^e\ge2e\), proving (7); \(e=0\) is immediate. \(\square\)

This requires neither universality nor computability.

### Theorem 3: sharpness for every universal \(M\)

For every fixed universal \(M\) and deterministic maximizing tie rule, there are \(B_M<\infty\) and an infinite sequence \(\alpha\) such that, for every \(n\ge1\),
\[
E_M(\epsilon,\alpha_{1:n})=n,
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
n\le
\log_2\frac{M(\epsilon)}{M(\alpha_{1:n})}
\le n+B_M.
}
\tag{8}
\]
Thus no coefficient smaller than \(1\) can replace the coefficient in the logarithmic bound, even with a fixed additive error.

**Proof.** Define
\[
\alpha_i=1-P_M(\alpha_{<i}).
\]
Every prediction is wrong, so Theorem 2 gives
\[
M(\alpha_{1:n})\le M(\epsilon)2^{-n}.
\]
Universality also supplies \(a_M>0\) such that
\[
M(w)\ge a_M2^{-|w|}
\]
for every finite \(w\). Hence
\[
M(\alpha_{1:n})\ge a_M2^{-n}.
\]
Taking logarithms proves (8), with
\[
B_M=\log_2\frac{M(\epsilon)}{a_M}.
\]

If \(E_M\le a\log_2R_M+B\) held uniformly with \(0\le a<1\), these witnesses would give
\[
n\le a(n+B_M)+B
\]
for all \(n\), a contradiction. Negative coefficients are excluded immediately. \(\square\)

The infinite sequence need not be computable. Each finite witness can separately be hardcoded and extended by zeros, so this is also a valid finite-witness statement when one requires computable environments.

**Confidence:** high. **Novelty:** the halving argument is standard; the stated sharpness is an elementary consequence. No priority claim.

## 3. A fixed universal AI prior with large separation on its own histories

The earlier reverse-comparison example did not establish that the relevant histories could arise under the same prior’s greedy policy. The following construction does.

### Theorem 4: mass inflation on the agent’s own greedy path

Fix any universal lower semicomputable chronological semimeasure \(B\) with \(B(\epsilon)=1\). There is one universal chronological semimeasure \(A\), effectively constructed from \(B\), with the following property.

For **every** deterministic maximizing tie rule, let \(\alpha_i\) be the agent’s actions when every reward is \(1\), and write
\[
h_n=(\alpha_1,1)\cdots(\alpha_n,1).
\]
Then, for **every** universal sequence semimeasure \(M\),
\[
A(h_n)\ge\frac14
\]
and
\[
\sum_{n\ge1}M(\alpha_{1:n})<\infty.
\tag{9}
\]
Consequently,
\[
\boxed{
\frac{A(h_n)}{M(\alpha_{1:n})}\to\infty,
\qquad
\frac{A(h_n)}{nM(\alpha_{1:n})}\to\infty.
}
\tag{10}
\]

These are actual greedy histories for the truth prefixes \(\alpha_{1:n}\).

### Construction

Let \(O\) be the always-successful environment:
\[
O(h)=
\begin{cases}
1,&\text{all rewards in \(h\) are \(1\)},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

Set \(R(h)=0\) whenever \(h\) contains a zero reward. On an all-one history, set
\[
R(h)=
\min\left(
1,\,
B(h_{i-1}(1-a_i,1)):1\le i\le |h|
\right).
\tag{11}
\]
Define
\[
\boxed{A=\frac{B+2R+O}{4}.}
\tag{12}
\]

### Proof

Finite minima preserve lower semicomputability. Moreover, on all-one histories,
\[
R(h(a,1))=\min\{R(h),B(h(1-a,1))\},
\qquad R(h(a,0))=0.
\]
Thus \(R\) is chronological. The mixture \(A\) is chronological, lower semicomputable, and universal because \(A\ge B/4\).

Along the all-success path, maintain the invariant
\[
B(h_n)\le R(h_n).
\tag{13}
\]
It holds at the root. At a history satisfying it, write
\[
b_a=B(h(a,1)).
\]
Since \(b_a\le B(h)\le R(h)\), the minimum in the child value does not truncate:
\[
R(h(a,1))=b_{1-a}.
\]
Therefore
\[
A(h(a,1))=\frac{b_a+2b_{1-a}+1}{4},
\]
and
\[
A(h(0,1))-A(h(1,1))=\frac{b_1-b_0}{4}.
\tag{14}
\]

Thus greedy \(A\) chooses an action minimizing the two \(B\)-success scores. If \(a\) is selected,
\[
B(h(a,1))=b_a\le b_{1-a}=R(h(a,1)),
\]
which propagates the invariant.

Now fix universal \(M\), and choose \(c_M>0\) such that
\[
B(g)\ge c_MM(d(g))
\]
for every history \(g\).

At cycle \(i\), put \(a=\alpha_i\), \(b=1-a\). The action-reversal property gives
\[
B(h_{i-1}(b,1))\ge B(h_i).
\]
The counterfactual history \(h_{i-1}(b,0)\) decodes to the actual truth prefix \(\alpha_{1:i}\). Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
B(h_{i-1})
&\ge B(h_{i-1}(b,1))+B(h_{i-1}(b,0))\\
&\ge B(h_i)+c_MM(\alpha_{1:i}).
\end{aligned}
\]
Telescoping yields
\[
c_M\sum_{i=1}^nM(\alpha_{1:i})\le1.
\tag{15}
\]

The sequence \(u_n=M(\alpha_{1:n})\) is nonincreasing. Summability gives
\[
nu_n\le2\sum_{i=\lceil n/2\rceil}^n u_i\longrightarrow0.
\]
Meanwhile \(A(h_n)\ge O(h_n)/4=1/4\), proving (10). \(\square\)

The same argument also gives the explicit finite estimate
\[
\frac{A(h_n)}{M(\alpha_{1:n})}\ge \frac{c_Mn}{4}.
\tag{16}
\]
The sequence \(\alpha\) must be noncomputable; otherwise universality would give a positive lower bound on all its prefix masses, contradicting (15).

### A single universal machine realizes this construction

Choose \(B\) from a base machine universal in both the chronological and ordinary sequence interfaces. Realize \(R\) by drawing one uniform real \(u\) from random program bits. After each action, emit reward \(1\) once
\[
u<R(h)
\]
is certified using finitely many bits of \(u\) and an enumerated rational lower bound for \(R(h)\). Otherwise wait. The thresholds decrease along extensions, so the probability of generating an all-one history is exactly \(R(h)\).

Two initial program bits select
\[
00:B,\qquad 01\text{ or }10:R,\qquad 11:O.
\]
The \(00\) branch simulates the base machine in both interfaces. The resulting SP prior is therefore universal, and Theorem 4 applies to that same-machine SP prior. The machine is fixed independently of \(n\).

**Confidence:** high; the construction and proof were independently checked. **Novelty:** construction supplied here; publication priority is not established.

### Why this does not refute (6.3N)

Every prediction on the witness truth \(\alpha\) is correct. Its error count is zero.

At the first zero reward, both \(R\) and \(O\) disappear:
\[
A(g)=B(g)/4
\]
on that history and all descendants. That first error loses at least \(1/4\) of AI mass, because the \(O/4\) component alone disappears. Thus this construction does not turn its inflated mass into many cheap mistakes.

It does rule out the proof route
\[

It even rules out uniform comparisons with \(mM(x)\), or with \(2^{K(m)}M(x)\). For the latter, take \(m=2^r\): then

whereas (16) gives a ratio of order at least \(2^r\).

Those are failures of **mass comparison**, not failures of the proposed error bounds.

### Theorem 5: normalization-index refinement

For every fixed universal \(A,M\), there is \(C\) such that
\[

\le

\tag{17}
\]
for all \(x\) and nonempty \(y\).

First, construct uniformly lower semicomputable conditional semimeasures \(T(v\mid x,j)\) satisfying
\[
2^jM(x)\le1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T(v\mid x,j)=2^jM(xv).
\tag{18}
\]
Take increasing rational semimeasure approximants \(M_s\uparrow M\). Follow \(2^jM_s(x\cdot)\) until its root first strictly exceeds \(1\), then freeze at the preceding whole semimeasure. This preserves lower semicomputability and root mass at most \(1\). If the limiting root is at most \(1\), no freeze occurs.

Such rational approximants can be obtained by taking finite-tree rational lower bounds and, working upwards, replacing each node by the maximum of its own lower bound and the sum of its children.

Now set
\[
Q(v\mid x)=\sum_j2^{-K(j\mid x)}T(v\mid x,j).
\]
Conditional Kraft gives root mass at most \(1\). The \(j=q(x)\) term satisfies
\[
\boxed{
Q(v\mid x)\ge
\frac12\,2^{-K(q(x)\mid x)}
\frac{M(xv)}{M(x)}.
}
\tag{19}
\]

Second, for each \(m\), construct a chronological environment that follows \(A\) through cycle \(m\), then uses \(Q(\cdot\mid d(h_m))\), multiplied by the accumulated mass \(A(h_m)\). Mix these environments with weights \(2^{-K(m)}\).

Universal domination, the error-budget proof, and the terminal mass yield
\[
(E_A(x,y)+1)c\,2^{-K(m)}A(h_x)Q(y\mid x)
\le A(h_x).
\]
Cancel \(A(h_x)>0\) and apply (19). \(\square\)

This is the sharper form behind the previous \((m+1)2^{K(m)}\) correction. Fair-coin domination bounds \(q(x)\le m+b\), and \(q(x)\) can therefore be encoded in \(\log_2(m+1)+O(1)\) bits given \(x\).

Consequently, for every fixed \(D_0\), there is \(C_{D_0}\) such that

The same conclusion holds under bounded maximal-prefix-complexity deficiency

for every fixed \(D\), there is \(C_D\) such that

\boxed{

\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E_A(x,y)+1\le C_D2^{K(m)}R_M(x,y).

\tag{22}

The coding theorem therefore gives

## 5. A matching linear obstruction for effective normalization

The following supplies a matching necessary correction for a specific method. It is **not** a necessary correction for AI mistakes.

Fix a computable prefix-free encoding \(e(w)\) with
\[
|e(w)|=|w|+O(\log(|w|+2)),
\]
and put
\[
f(j)=0^j1.
\]

### Theorem 8: bounded-ratio tails with hard effective normalization

For every fixed universal \(M\), there is \(a>0\) such that
\[
\boxed{
\frac{M(e(w)f(K(w)))}{M(e(w))}\ge a
\qquad(\forall w).
}
\tag{27}
\]

For every uniformly lower semicomputable conditional binary semimeasure \(Q(v\mid x)\), there is \(C_Q\) such that, for every sufficiently large \(n\), some \(w\in\{0,1\}^n\) satisfies
\[
\boxed{
Q(f(K(w))\mid e(w))\le \frac{C_Q}{n}.
}
\tag{28}
\]
These witnesses can also satisfy \(K(w)\ge n/2-O(1)\).

Consequently, no such \(Q\) can satisfy
\[
Q(y\mid x)\ge
\frac{c}{g(|x|)}\frac{M(xy)}{M(x)}
\quad(\forall x,y)
\tag{29}
\]
for a fixed \(c>0\) and any positive function \(g(t)=o(t)\).

**Proof of (27).** For any computable prefix-free encoding,
\[
M(e(w))\asymp2^{-K(w)}.
\]
The upper bound follows because \(w\mapsto M(e(w))\) is a discrete semimeasure; the lower bound follows by running a shortest prefix program and printing its encoded output.

The concatenated code \((w,j)\mapsto e(w)f(j)\) is also prefix-free. Hence
\[
M(e(w)f(K(w)))\asymp2^{-K(w,K(w))}.
\]
But
\[
K(w,K(w))=K(w)+O(1):
\]
a fixed wrapper around a shortest prefix program outputs its result and its program length; projection proves the reverse inequality. This proves (27). \(\square\)

**External input for (28).** Bauwens and Shen’s Theorem 2 gives a constant \(c_0\) such that, for every \(n\), some \(n\)-bit \(w\) satisfies
\[
K(K(w)\mid w)\ge\log_2n-c_0,
\qquad
K(w)\ge n/2-c_0.
\tag{30}
\]
Their theorem states a stronger plain conditional-complexity lower bound, which implies the displayed prefix-complexity bound. I use this established theorem as an external input; I am not claiming a new proof of it. [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/1202.6668v2?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Proof of (28) and (29).** Prefix-freeness of \(f\) gives
\[
\sum_jQ(f(j)\mid e(w))\le1.
\]
Thus these values form a uniformly lower semicomputable conditional discrete semimeasure. Conditional coding gives
\[
K(j\mid w)
\le-\log_2Q(f(j)\mid e(w))+O_Q(1).
\]
Choose \(w\) from (30) and set \(j=K(w)\). Rearranging proves (28).

Now \(|e(w)|=n+O(\log n)\). The uniformly positive lower bound in (27) and the \(O(1/n)\) upper bound in (28) contradict (29) whenever \(g(t)=o(t)\). \(\square\)

The tails here have length
\[
|f(K(w))|=K(w)+1=\Theta(n)
\]
on the selected witnesses.

### Theorem 9: matching linear upper bound

For every universal \(M\), there are a uniformly lower semicomputable conditional semimeasure \(Q_0\) and a nonnegative integer \(b\) such that

\]

Since \(2^{q(x)}M(x)>1/2\), that single term proves (31). \(\square\)

Thus the worst-case loss for uniformly effective, truth-prefix-conditioned normalization is **linear**, with matching upper and lower bounds.
